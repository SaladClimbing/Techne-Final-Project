Where Do Goals Come From? Exploring NHL Shot Quality Over 19 Seasons

Author

Andrew Lucas

Introduction

How have NHL shot profiles and scoring rates changed across 19 seasons (2007–2025), and what shot characteristics best predict goals? This analysis uses shot-level play-by-play data from MoneyPuck spanning 2,079,377 shot events to examine the evolution of shot selection in the modern NHL, evaluate an expected goals (xG) model, and identify which shot characteristics most strongly predict scoring.

The so-called “analytics revolution” in hockey has emphasized shot quality over shot quantity. We investigate whether the data supports this narrative by tracking how shot distances, angles, types, and rush/rebound rates have shifted over time.

The dataset contains 2,079,359 shot events across 19 seasons, with 140,157 goals. Two seasons had shortened schedules due to work stoppages: 2012 (NHL lockout, 48 games) and 2020 (COVID-19, 56 games). We flag these for normalization in later analyses.

Simple Logistic Regression Model

The logistic regression achieves a deviance of 9.17487^{5} on 2,074,837 residual degrees of freedom. Key findings:

  • Shot distance has a strong negative coefficient: each additional foot from the net reduces the log-odds of a goal.
  • Shot angle has a moderate negative effect: wider angles (shots from the periphery) are less dangerous.
  • Rush shots and rebounds substantially increase goal probability.
  • Snap shots and tips/deflections outperform wrist shots, while slap shots perform similarly.

Conclusion

This analysis reveals several key findings about NHL shot quality:

  1. Scoring rates have been relatively stable over 19 seasons, hovering around 9–10% per shot, with no dramatic league-wide trend despite the analytics revolution narrative.

  2. Shot location dominates scoring probability. The slot (close range, centered angle) remains by far the most dangerous area. Shots from inside 20 feet convert at nearly three times the rate of shots from 30+ feet.

  3. Rush and rebound situations are high-value. Rush shots and rebounds produce meaningfully higher goal rates than standard shots, suggesting that transition play and second-chance opportunities are critical to scoring.

  4. The xG model is reasonably well-calibrated, though it tends to slightly underpredict goals at the highest probability levels.

  5. Individual shooter deviations from xG reveal potential over- and under-performers, though isolating this from contextual factors (quality of chances, team system) would require additional analysis.

Limitations

  • The 2012 lockout and 2020 COVID seasons had fewer games; per-shot rates are used to mitigate this, but per-game comparisons should be made cautiously.
  • The xG model is a black box from MoneyPuck. We evaluate its output but cannot inspect its internals.
  • No game-state context (score differential, time remaining) is incorporated into our simple model.
  • The data includes regular season and playoff games without distinction in most analyses (though isPlayoffGame is available).
